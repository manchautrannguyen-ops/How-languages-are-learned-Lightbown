The behaviourist perspective

As seen in Chapter 1, behaviourist theory explained learning as imitation, practice, reinforcement, and habit formation. Early research used laboratory animals but the same process was assumed for humans.

Second language applications: Mimicry and memorization

Behaviourism dominated L2 teaching in North America from the 1940s–1970s. Nelson Brooks (1960) and Robert Lado (1964) were key proponents, influencing the development of audiolingual teaching materials emphasizing (4) , memorization, dialogues, and sentence patterns. This led to the rejection of both CAH and behaviourism by the 1970s—causing a period when the role of the L1 and the role of practice were neglected. This is far more sophisticated than habit formation. As research evolved, some behaviourist explanations have been revisited and (5) within newer learning theories.

The innatist perspective

Chomsky's critique of behaviourism (largely in his critical review of Skinner) led to the proposal that innate knowledge of Universal Grammar (UG) allows children to acquire their L1 during a (6) period. Although Chomsky didn't make specific claims about L2, linguists like Lydia White (2003) have argued UG also offers the best framework for understanding SLA.

Others (e.g., Bley-Vroman 1990; Schachter 1990) argue UG works for L1 but not for L2—especially after the critical period—and that SLA requires a different (more general psychological) theory.

  2. Monitor hypothesis: In (16) communication, learners rely on the acquired system. The learned system acts as an "editor" or "monitor" making minor corrections—but only when speakers have time, focus on form, and know the relevant rules.

  4. Comprehensible input hypothesis: Acquisition happens when learners are exposed to comprehensible input containing i + 1—language a step beyond their current level (i).

The competition model

Elizabeth Bates and Brian MacWhinney (1981) explain both L1 and L2 acquisition by considering language form, meaning, and use together. Speakers learn to use cues signaling specific functions: word order, (43) markers, and the animacy of nouns. Languages differ in which cues are primary.

English relies heavily on word order (SVO). English-speaking children initially use animacy and world knowledge (a two-year-old hearing "Box push boy" makes the boy push the box). By age four, they follow SVO so strongly they may make the box push the boy. They later learn passive markers.

English speakers learning these languages may struggle to (45) reliance on word order; conversely, Italian speakers may use animacy rather than word order to interpret odd English sentences.

Drawing on Vygotsky (Chapter 1), sociocultural theory holds that cognitive development—including language development—arises through social interactions.
