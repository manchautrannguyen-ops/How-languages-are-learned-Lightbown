The first three years: Milestones and developmental sequences

There is remarkable similarity in children's early language development worldwide. Newborns can distinguish human speech sounds from other sounds, recognize their mother's voice, and (3) subtle phonetic distinctions (e.g., "pa" vs. "ba"), even sounds not used in their native language. By around 12 months, most children produce their first recognizable words; by age two, they produce many words and begin combining them into "telegraphic" sentences (e.g., "Mommy juice," "baby fall down") that follow proper word order and show creative combinations rather than mere (4). The development follows predictable patterns called developmental sequences or "stages."

Grammatical morphemes: Roger Brown's longitudinal study found that 14 grammatical morphemes (e.g., present progressive -ing, plural -s, possessive -s, articles, past tense, third person -s) are acquired in a similar order across children, though at different ages. Negation: Develops in four stages, from single "no" + word ("No cookie") to inserting negatives before verbs, then attaching to (6), and finally using correct forms across persons and tenses.

The pre-school years

By age four, children can ask questions, give commands, report events, and create stories using correct word order most of the time. Three- and four-year-olds add several new words daily and acquire complex structures like passives and relative clauses. They learn to use language in widening social contexts—adjusting how they speak to babies versus adults—and begin developing (8) awareness, the ability to treat language as an object separate from meaning (e.g., recognizing that "drink the chair" is "silly"). Children spend roughly 20,000+ hours in language-rich environments by the time they start school.

The school years

Vocabulary growth is the most impressive development—children enter school knowing several thousand words and add several hundred to over a thousand each year, largely through reading (especially varied text types including non-fiction). They learn different language registers (how spoken differs from written; how one speaks to a (9) versus on the playground; narrative versus science writing). Children who speak (10), regional, or different language varieties must also learn the standard variety used in school. Metalinguistic awareness expands greatly through learning to read.

Explaining first language acquisition

While what children learn is well documented, how they learn it remains controversial. Three main theoretical perspectives have been (11) since the mid-20th century: behaviourist, innatist, and interactionist/developmental.

The behaviourist perspective

Most associated with B. F. Skinner (1957), this 1940s–50s theory holds that language is learned through (12), practice, reinforcement, and habit formation. Children imitate what they hear; correct attempts get positive reinforcement (praise or successful communication), forming habits. The environment is the source of everything the child needs to learn. However, while imitation and practice explain some early vocabulary and routines, they cannot account for children's creative, rule-governed productions (e.g., "goed," "feets") or for the complex grammar children acquire without being (13) taught.

The innatist perspective

Noam Chomsky challenged behaviourism, arguing children are biologically programmed for language, which develops like other (14) functions (e.g., walking). He pointed to the "logical problem of language acquisition"—children come to know more about language than they could learn from the limited and imperfect input they hear. He proposed children are born with Universal Grammar (UG): innate knowledge of principles common to all languages. The complexity of grammar (illustrated by (15) pronouns like "himself") and the fact that virtually all children—including deaf children exposed to sign language and children with limited (16) ability—acquire complex language systems support this view.

The innatist perspective is linked to the Critical Period Hypothesis (CPH), which proposes there is a biological window in early childhood during which language must be acquired. Cases of extreme (17) (Victor, the "wild boy of Aveyron"; Genie, isolated until age 13) provide partial but inconclusive support. Stronger evidence comes from studies of deaf signers: Elissa Newport's research showed Native, Early, and Late ASL learners differed in (18) of grammatical markers, supporting a critical period regardless of whether language is oral or gestural. Research on international adoptees and neurological studies suggests language acquisition begins at or even before birth.

Interactionist/developmental perspectives

This view emphasizes the interplay between children's innate learning ability and their environment. Developmental psychologists argue innatists (19) the "final state" and underemphasize the developmental process. Language acquisition is seen as similar to other learning, not driven by a separate brain module.

Language disorders and delays

Most children develop language without difficulty, but some have (26) (deafness, articulation issues, autism, dyslexia) that affect language development. The "normal" range is wide. Parents and teachers should seek professional advice when concerned, but should be careful not to confuse normal individual differences—or limited knowledge of the school's language—with disorders. Bilingual children with limited (27) in the school language are sometimes misidentified as having delays.

Childhood bilingualism

Bilingualism is the norm globally, not the exception. Children who learn two languages from (28) are simultaneous bilinguals; those who add a language later are sequential bilinguals. Contrary to popular myth, learning two languages does not confuse children or significantly slow development. Bilingualism can enhance metalinguistic awareness and cognitive flexibility (Bialystok). Code-(29) (using words from multiple languages in one conversation) is a normal, patterned, often (30) feature of bilingual speech—not a sign of deficiency.

A key distinction:

Additive bilingualism: Adding a second language while maintaining the first—the recommended approach.

Subtractive bilingualism: Losing the first language as the second is acquired—often harmful, leading to weaker proficiency in both languages, academic struggles, and (31) family relationships.

Jim Cummins distinguished BICS (Basic Interpersonal Communication Skills—everyday conversational language) from CALP (Cognitive Academic Language Proficiency—the more difficult, (32) language needed for school success). Maintaining the home language while learning the school language is strongly supported by research.
